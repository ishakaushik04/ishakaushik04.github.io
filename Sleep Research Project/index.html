The problem

PTSD care arrives years late.

Most people exposed to trauma never develop PTSD. The ones who do wait a long time for help, so we need an early, cheap signal for who is at risk.

The mechanism

Sleep is the signal.

Insomnia and nightmares sit at the centre of PTSD. REM sleep and noradrenaline (NA) feed each other: NA has to drop for REM to form, and when REM is lost, brain NA climbs and drives many of the symptoms.

1 · TraumaStress response switches on
2 · Sleep breaksInsomnia, recurrent nightmares
3 · REM lostEmotional regulation weakens
4 · NA risesLocus coeruleus–NA pathway overactive
5 · HyperarousalWakefulness, more REM loss

↺ and back to 3. The loop sustains itself.

The framework

REMS–PTSD risk matrix.

A structured model for judging PTSD risk from sleep and neurochemical markers. Three parts, each with one job in assessment.

Trauma exposure and sleep dysregulation

Trauma triggers a stress response, often insomnia and recurrent nightmares. Chronic sleep problems weaken emotional regulation and raise the odds of PTSD.

Use it to: flag persistent sleep disruption early.

The noradrenaline mechanism

PTSD overactivates the sympathetic system, including the locus coeruleus–NA pathway. Low Neuropeptide Y and less sensitive alpha-2 autoreceptors amplify NA, causing hyperarousal, wakefulness and REM disruption.

Use it to: track REM sleep quality and stress biomarkers to gauge neurobiological vulnerability.

Individual susceptibility

  • Age: older adults may cope less well
  • Sex: women show higher susceptibility
  • Epigenetics: can predispose to stronger stress responses

Use it to: put risk in context and personalise the evaluation.

In practice

A workflow for low-resource settings.

No neuroimaging, no lab. Four stages that a trauma center, community clinic or research team can adapt to what it has.

Screen sleep disruption

A brief check right after trauma. This is the window that matters, since under 10% get treatment in the first year.

  • Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (free, 5–10 min)
  • 0–10 sleep quality rating
  • Nightmare frequency log
  • 3–7 day sleep diary

Watch for two signals: trouble falling or staying asleep, and recurrent distressing dreams.

Stratify risk

For people with persistent sleep problems, add the susceptibility factors. It takes clinical history, not expensive tests. Try the scoring below.

01 point per box. 3 or more means closer monitoring and early intervention.

Social support is assessed too, as a protective factor, and isn't scored here. This shows the framework's proposed logic and is not a clinical tool.

Use proxies for REM disruption

Polysomnography isn't always available. These stand in for REM disruption and noradrenergic hyperactivity.

  • Dream recall frequency
  • Nightmare timing (early morning, REM-dense)
  • Sleep fragmentation
  • Resting heart rate
  • Hypervigilance rating
  • Startle severity
  • Wearable HRV, if available

Consumer sleep trackers (about $50–200) give objective fragmentation data that tracks with REM disruption.

Target the noradrenergic hyperactivity that keeps breaking REM sleep and feeding PTSD symptoms.

Scale it by what you have

Tier 1 · Minimal

Stages 1–2 on paper, basic risk scoring, referral protocol for high-risk cases.

Tier 2 · Moderate

Add consumer wearables, structured interventions and a follow-up schedule.

Tier 3 · Research

Full polysomnography, biomarkers such as salivary cortisol, controlled trials.

Track four things, even in a spreadsheet: initial risk score, sleep metrics over time, interventions given, and outcomes at 3, 6 and 12 months.

Why it matters

Sleep problems are observable, measurable and treatable. That makes them a good place to catch PTSD in weeks, not years.

The aim is actionable assessment, not perfect assessment. For feedback and new versions, contact the co-authors.
