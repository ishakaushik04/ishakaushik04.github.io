REM Sleep Disruption and Noradrenergic Hyperactivity as a Risk Framework for PTSD

Isha Kaushik, Harshpreet Kaur

May to August 2022 · April to August 2023

Guides: Prof. Birendra Nath Mallick, Dr. Rachna Mehta, Dr. Vijay Kumar · Preprint (PDF)

In short

A proposed way to spot early PTSD risk through sleep problems, built from 500+ studies and designed for places without labs or brain scans. It is research-stage and has not been tested in patients.

AbstractMost people exposed to trauma do not develop PTSD, but those who do wait years for treatment. Insomnia and nightmares are central to the disorder. REM sleep and noradrenaline (NA) form a reciprocal loop: NA must fall for REM to occur, and REM loss raises brain NA, driving hyperarousal. From a review of 500+ studies (1970s to 2022) we derived a three-module framework that organizes sleep and neurochemical markers into a testable risk model, and a four-stage workflow for low-resource settings. The framework is literature-derived and has not been validated in patients or clinics.

Background

Reported rates, scaled to 1,000 people (Fig. 1a): 70% experience a potentially traumatic event; 5.6% of those develop PTSD (about 39; the global figure is 3.9%); at most 40% recover within a year; fewer than 1 in 10 receive treatment in the first year. Average time to treatment is 4.5 years, and the median reaches 12 years in some settings (Fig. 1b). An early, low-cost signal of risk could shorten this interval.

Mechanism

NA must decline for REM sleep to form, so any rise in NA makes REM harder to obtain. Loss of REM weakens emotional regulation and raises brain NA through an overactive locus coeruleus-NA pathway. Lower neuropeptide Y (NPY) and less sensitive alpha-2 autoreceptors amplify this step. The resulting hyperarousal and wakefulness cause further REM loss, so the loop sustains itself (Fig. 2).

Discussion

Sleep problems are observable, measurable and treatable, which makes them a candidate source of early PTSD risk signals. The aim is actionable assessment, not perfect assessment.

Limitations

The framework is derived from the literature and has not been validated in patients or clinics. The workflow and the scoring threshold are untested, and Fig. 1a and Fig. 2 are illustrative. For feedback and new versions, contact the co-authors.
